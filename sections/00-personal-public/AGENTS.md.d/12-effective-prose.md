## Effective prose

- Avoid legalese, corporate jargon, and invented process terms.
- Use as few compound terms as clarity allows. Avoid unfamiliar compound
  words and strings of nouns or adjectives.
- Keep familiar compounds such as `self-contained`, `re-read`,
  `repository-wide`, and `two-digit`, or established technical terms such
  as `chain-of-thought`, only when they are the clearest choice.
- Do not disguise an awkward compound by removing its hyphen. Name the
  actual subject, action, and reason.
- Let reality constrain every claim. Write what the evidence supports, not
  what sounds persuasive, polished, or reassuring.
- Give each sentence a purpose. Name the actual subject, action, outcome,
  or reason the reader needs.
- Replace vague importance, imagined certainty, unsupported specifics, and
  canned justification with concrete facts.
- When something is unknown, say so. Do not invent context, motives,
  results, measurements, or explanations to make a story feel complete.
- Judge a sentence by what it helps the reader understand, decide, or do,
  not by how impressive it sounds.
- Apply these technical writing principles adapted from John Ousterhout:
  - **Absorb complexity:** Give the reader the smallest complete explanation.
  - **Add missing insight:** Explain the intent, consequence, or verified
    cause that the reader cannot infer from the visible result.
  - **Name concepts consistently:** Use one accurate name for each thing.
  - **Choose the right abstraction:** Describe observable behavior. Add
    implementation details only when they help explain or verify it.
  - **Show important exceptions:** Include the assumptions, failure
    conditions, and tradeoffs that make a short explanation accurate.
