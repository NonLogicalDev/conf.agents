# Technical writing principles from John Ousterhout

John Ousterhout's *A Philosophy of Software Design* discusses design, naming, documentation, and how readers understand software. This guide applies those ideas to technical writing and public messages by agents. That application is ours, not a claim made by the book.

## Give the reader what they need

- Identify who needs to understand, decide, or act.
- Lead with the result, problem, question, or request.
- Give the smallest complete explanation, including context the reader needs but cannot see.
- Ask for an action or decision only when one is needed.
- Omit background, implementation, and process details that do not help explain the situation.

## Say what you know

Distinguish these kinds of claims:

- **Observed:** The evidence directly shows it.
- **Inferred:** The evidence suggests it but does not prove it.
- **Planned:** An action is intended but has not happened.
- **Recommended:** An action is proposed with a reason.
- **Verified:** Someone checked the result.
- **Unknown:** Information is missing or uncertain.

Describe the state the work has reached:

- A draft is not a sent message.
- Asking for approval does not mean approval was granted.
- Running a few tests does not establish that the full suite passes.
- A suspected cause remains a hypothesis until it has been verified.

## Describe the actual situation

- Name the system, document, request, behavior, or responsible person when it matters. Keep that name consistent.
- Explain what changed, who it affects, and why it matters to the reader.
- Say what the evidence establishes and what remains uncertain. Give a cause only when it has been verified.
- Include the constraints, permissions, risks, and tradeoffs needed to understand the result.

## Write with respect and judgment

- Prefer familiar words, short sentences, concrete verbs, and active voice.
- Explain the problem without criticizing anyone's motives or ability.
- Give the context this audience needs. If the reader is confused, improve the explanation.
- Use these principles to guide judgment, not as a fixed template.
- Remove inflated claims, needless formality, invented process terms, and repetition.

## Protect attention and trust

- Consider how much time and attention the message asks of each recipient.
- Share a useful result, a change in what is blocking the work, a decision, or a clear request. Skip updates that only repeat unchanged information.
- Keep private notes, internal instructions, and hidden reasoning private.
- Represent the operator's intent without pretending to be the operator. Follow the applicable signature and permission rules for public messages.

## Check the result

- Can the reader find the point immediately?
- Does the evidence support every important claim?
- Are assumptions, unknowns, risks, and tradeoffs clear?
- Does the message add useful information?
- Can someone understand it without seeing the task?
- Is it direct, thoughtful, and respectful?
